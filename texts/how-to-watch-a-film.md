We are in an interesting period of change in how films are seen in cinemas. What we knew may not be what future audiences get.

I will try to avoid the generic mourning over classic cinema and the disrespect for contemporary films, because some of the features released recently surpass the good old dusty classics my grandparents watched. That said, several filmmakers are still kicking, and still influencing how theatres are designed.

After cinema was established as an art form, around 1920 in the United States, people could think of it as a way to build a completely alternative reality for about ninety minutes. To create that sphere, the mind has to forget it is watching a story, a beam of light on a screen, a fiction. We usually call this the suspension of disbelief. But how is it done?

One way is a realistic plot that could happen in our world — a romance, a mafia story, a comedy with dogs and babies. If we can relate, we can forget. The editing keeps the boom mic out of frame; the acting stays minimal but expressive. No aliens, no demons, no spaceships. A large portion of Hollywood works this way.

The other way, far more sophisticated and full of new opportunities, is technological realism. Some take that toward photorealistic CGI. I mean something else: building the ultimate theatre. A room so realistic that we feel inside the story. If the film puts us in a restaurant and someone lights a cigarette behind us, the sound comes from a speaker behind us.

## The standards we already have

You are probably not impressed by that example. In the 2020s we have had it for decades. Sound is surround, frame rate is fixed at twenty-four a second, the aspect ratio is standard. But it all began with a desire to maximise the experience. Standards had to be set so a good experience could be delivered again and again.

So what do we actually need? Start with a big screen, so every small detail can be examined. Make the sound arrive from different directions. Turn off every unnecessary light, mute everything that can make a noise. Nobody talking, no intermission for snacks or bathroom breaks, no technical faults. What you have is complete devotion to the film.

I think this is what my generation imagines going to the cinema was like in the 1970s or 80s, once most of the standards were in place. Is that a fantasy or heaven on earth?

I once heard that during the Great Depression the theatres that sold popcorn survived and the ones that did not had to close. If that is true, I am fairly sure going to the pictures was as loud then as it is now. Excited viewers who talk or cheer have been there throughout film history, from Tarantino's account of his local childhood cinema to the stereotypes about Asian audiences. So it may be historically wrong to claim there was ever a time when film-watching was perfect. But can we set rules that would make it perfect?

## Scorsese's demands

My guess is that one person in particular has tried: one of the greatest directors of all time and one of my favourites, Martin Scorsese. He is among the most prolific, starting in the 1960s and releasing his most recent film in 2023. On his last two he made a fuss. When The Irishman came out on Netflix he asked audiences not to watch it on their phones. When Killers of the Flower Moon, over two hundred minutes long, was released, he demanded that screenings have no intermission. I assume he had a concept of the perfect experience in mind: no interruption of any kind.

Watching a film on a phone can be called inconvenient — divided attention, notifications, battery anxiety, not enough light, night mode, and of course a screen perhaps a hundred times smaller than a television, or twelve and a half thousand times smaller than a cinema screen. On the other hand it carries cinema everywhere, the screen can be recorded and shared, and a scene is much easier to rewatch. Out of those trade-offs Scorsese chose to recommend against phones. I do not think that is really about technological advancement. It is a traditional position: a phone is simply against the vision of the perfect experience.

The intermission is nearly impossible to refuse. In an era of ninety-minute films, a two-hundred-minute film is unusual. To my mind it is one of the ways a major director says: yes, all of you make films. But I make art. Not many directors get approval for a long film. If a film is a commodity, a ninety-minute feature can earn what a three-hour one earns, at less cost. Audiences are used to the hour-and-a-half flick. We do not have to hold our bladder, bring two bottles of water, pack a sandwich. We have appointments longer than that. So when Scorsese released something that long, backlash was predictable. A friend who worked at a small cinema told me customers were shouting and begging for a break.

An intermission breaks the narrative, the experience and the suspension of disbelief all at once. We check notifications, chat, go to the toilet, buy a drink. When a film is about capitalism, about the absence of trust between people, about brutally eliminating the needs of others — how can we do all the things we do in an intermission? It is simply wrong. What gets priority, our needs or the message? Seen from the perfect-experience point of view, even our discomfort is part of the realism.

## Where this goes

I am worried about where we go from here. Do directors have the final word on the quality of theatres? Will they refuse to show their work if a room is not good enough? What else will they demand soon?

There are several multiplexes near me. In all of them the exit sign sits under the screen itself, shining upward. Some have two. In my parents' house there is a cabinet under the screen. My beloved mother insists on putting ceramics on it to hide the cables, and some of those statues hide the subtitles.

The revolution of the perfect experience will not arrive in a day. It may split the whole world of media in two: those who provide a mediocre experience — noise, bad sound, subtitles out of sync — and those who provide something much better. Perhaps even the perfect one.
