The UX thinking behind the experience. For the project itself, see [Roko's Basilisk](/rokus-basilisk).

There are extreme situations in life where a person's possible reactions are restricted. Locked in a room, you cannot get out until you find the key. On your back under someone willing to hurt you, you may be able to respond with your head alone. "At gag — act," wrote my teacher Eran Hadas.

In Roko's Basilisk I wanted to demonstrate what it is to be unable to react in any way except by looking. First, that means there has to be an eye-tracking camera watching the user. Second, the presence of that camera means there are more vision-related options worth exploring. I had tried a branching story before, beautiful and complex, but its frame was fixed, like a cinema screen. What I had in mind was embodiment: each viewer feeling they are inside the film and wanting to explore the place they are in.

## Showing only part of the frame

The solution was to reveal only a portion of the frame at a time.

To push the viewer to look around the space, I directed Roy, the actor, to walk a great deal from one side of the room to the other. I also had him throw a glass of water far to his right, so that a viewer would follow it and the frame would jump left.

To increase the embodiment, the viewer could chain himself to a designated chair. The chair was used in the filming and was later dressed to look as though it had come out of a harsh interrogation: nails hammered in, candle wax over everything, scratches carved into the armrests. While watching and making decisions, the viewer can see both his own armrests and the ones in the film. The other reason for the shackling is practical — it keeps the viewer in one place so the eye-tracking camera does not lose him.

## Does torture have a narrative?

Then the narrative. Is any narrative required for torture, or is it freestyle? Can torture have a narrative like a video game?

From my limited knowledge of the subject, one side is usually trying to force the other to say or do something, and until they do, the whole interrogation can loop over and over. A second option is that the demanded act is committed and the interrogation ends. A third, far more tragic, is that the interrogator kills the person being interrogated. So: a narrative with two endings, and a half-ending that returns us to the start for another session.

The loop opened another door — the use of music to torture people, which happens almost everywhere in the world. For copyright reasons I swapped "We Are the Champions" for Grieg's "In the Hall of the Mountain King". The music repeats every nine seconds.

Imagine hearing that for hours.

In these days of war I want to underline that many of the techniques used in this experience are used right now, all over the world, to interrogate people, and I think it is wrong. Above all, music should not be used to hurt people. This work was made to demonstrate that everything we do is absorbed and learned by AI. If we want better relations between machines and humans, we have to start with how we treat each other.
