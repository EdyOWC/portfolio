What is the vision of the media world today? By vision I mean a goal not currently achieved, which enough effort could accomplish.

In his book, Denis McQuail sets out five roles for mass media: to entertain, to report new information, to interpret the news, to transmit traditions, and to recruit audiences for various efforts. Every industry has a purpose like this. Modern agriculture wants to feed the world's entire population and wants that food to be excellent; for most of history humans starved, so the vision was relevant. Transport moves people to the ends of the earth; for most of history long-distance movement was complicated and dangerous, which gave that sector a good reason to develop. Academia provides education for good employment and expands what humanity knows.

The media world goes beyond mass communication, because its forms — films, interactive experiences, devices that combine pleasure — are not necessarily meant for wide distribution, at least not simultaneously, and can be personalised. If a single customer will pay enough, a creator can make them a media object that meets their needs and nobody else's. Media products are also art forms. So a simple comparison between the visions of the advanced media world and a widely distributed newspaper is beside the point. Still, it is a good place to start.

## Entertainment is already solved

Take McQuail's first purpose: entertainment. If the vision was to entertain a person successfully from the day of their birth to the day they die of old age, that has already been achieved.

One estimate puts the number of films produced since the beginning of cinema at about half a million. At a common length of ninety minutes, a person who started watching at birth would finish that selection at eighty-five. Today more films are made worldwide each year than can be watched in the same period — Hollywood, Bollywood and Nollywood produce around two thousand each, which is three hundred and seventy-five consecutive days of viewing.

The same holds elsewhere. An "only Bob Dylan" radio station can play all day without repeating a song, since he has between fifty-five and seventy hours of music. Every twenty-four hours, roughly thirty thousand days of viewing material is uploaded to YouTube; on Pornhub, five thousand days. Steam gets about fifty new games a day.

If the goal of media was only entertainment, the vision is met and a new one has to be found.

## Further, Other, New

I want to offer another way of looking at the visions of the media world, along three axes. On the **Further** axis an entity — a person, a studio, a nation — makes more media of the same type and content. On the **Other** axis it makes media of the same type with different content. On the **New** axis it makes something genuinely different that pushes the industry toward new territory.

The easiest demonstration is today's leading cinematic form: superheroes in franchises. A studio that produces more content about an existing superhero increases the amount of knowledge about that character and plays inside a playground with clear guidelines — the origin story, the themes of the character's world — and inside the industry's rules: ninety minutes or so, the cinematic means of expression, copyright law, labour law. Producing another such film, however successful, is Further.

Every so often a new superhero appears, with a different backstory and different themes. Content about that hero creates a fresh franchise, unbound by other characters' origins but still entirely bound by the rules of industry and genre. This is not an innovation that disrupts the superhero market; it is another product in the same market. My grandfather used to say "Dasselbe in Grün" — the same thing, only in green. Here is the superhero market of recent decades in the abstract: the enemy in the comics is no longer a Nazi but a Communist, an Asian or an Islamist, and the hero, whatever the colour of the suit, must defeat them and sell dolls in children's meals around the world. That is Other.

The last and most fascinating option is Completely New, and it is the one that offers real change. If there is a vision, it is here. Inventing the superhero genre was the innovation. The move away from unbranded toys of animals or cowboys, with no defined story and no marketing rights, is exactly what the genre offered eighty years ago. In the same way, Barbie arriving as a doll with specific looks and traits changed how girls everywhere play; since then children scream at their mothers for the toy of a specific brand. But make no mistake: only Superman and Barbie of the 1940s are New. Bratz and Spider-Man are Other, and the most recent media from all four franchises is Further.

Something that still surprises me is that many of my friends approaching thirty still attach importance to brands that were aimed at us as children. The Harry Potter franchise has not offered a new idea since the first book. It is the same struggle around racism and orphanhood — in the films, the games, the spin-off books, the toys, the new television series. Even the Newt Scamander films did not realise the Other option. To me, any further Harry Potter content is Further, and the interest behind more merchandise is purely commercial. I no longer attribute cultural importance to it.

One creator who genuinely worked on the Other axis is James Cameron. Over many years he founded several significant franchises, or made sequels that became them. Before The Terminator, thinking about human–machine relations rested on Blade Runner, Modern Times and Asimov's books, and there is a line between the terror in those and Cameron's films. The Avatar franchise connects to post-colonial thought and to Lawrence of Arabia, Dances with Wolves and Tarzan.

Innovation in media is rare, but it shows up in many forms: methods of production, distribution, technology, content, the invention of a genre. Which returns us to vision, because the aspiration to make more money by making more content is not an industry vision — it is the goal of one entity inside the industry.

George Lucas, conceiving Star Wars, worked on the Other axis. The 1977 film was marketed as a Western in space, and as such it is a window into a new franchise built on the rules of the Western. Its innovation came from a completely different direction. Despite offers to raise his salary, Lucas chose to take the full merchandise rights, which was revolutionary at the time and changed the film industry outright. There he acted on the New axis. In deciding to keep making films in the franchise, he acted on Further. Some of his aspirations across those years were financial, but those decisions led to far-reaching changes in the workplaces of a great many other people, not only in his own pocket.

## Recording everything

So far we have only been in the relations of entertainment and money. The personal wish to create is not reprehensible, but as long as it produces a new film for an old franchise, or a new franchise that imitates others, there is no innovation that could be called a vision. McQuail's second purpose — disseminating information — is not a future vision for the industry either.

If there are, across past, present and future, a finite number of perspectives that need presenting, one possible vision would be that we must represent them all: create an enormous quantity of content telling the lives and deeds of everyone who lived or could live. Hence Steve Jobs and Jobs, The Social Network, Milk, Lawrence of Arabia. Under that vision, media is nothing but a multitude of light beams that must present the entirety of human life at least once. By that logic fictional stories carry no real importance, and neither does the number of films about the same person — why should the founder of Apple get two while someone else gets none?

This vision fits very specific cases, such as collecting testimony of the Holocaust and of October 7th into archives. "We intend to document everyone who experienced these events" is a formulable vision, even if execution is very hard. For cinema it is simply pointless, because cinema is also art. It might find expression in a sub-genre of documentary, but media as a whole will not direct its resources there.

## History and the future

Detaching entirely from McQuail and mass communication, I would look at the near and slightly more distant past.

One of early cinema's first visions was to attach sound to the moving picture and so create an art in which characters can be heard speaking. That vision drove inventors and technicians to make something that did not yet exist, and it had consequences: a person could be filmed and their words preserved for later; films could be shown where the music matched the dancing. The visions that followed — with a good appetite, given how well the soundtrack had gone — were colour, effects, digitisation, and the ability to control a film while watching it: pause, rewind, change the subtitles.

These were significant, and they justified the funds and resources spent solving them. Beyond technology, the drive to build worldwide distribution mechanisms, animal rights on set, fair wages for crew and copyright registration were also visions that took shape and mattered.

More recently it seems that effects and the reduction of shooting days became the core of cinematic vision: 3D models, the move from on-set pyrotechnics to computer effects, whole shots generated inside a machine.

One possible vision is a film made by a single person. Assume this creator will let go of their ego, that there are no departments to manage, and that only their name appears in the credits. There is still a high chance they will need a good lawyer to explain ownership of all the voices of "actors" who are not credited. Another vision, advancing right now, is a film with no human touch at all — no script written by a person, no editing, no direction. It sounds illogical that a film made by artificial intelligence could exist without a single human prompt. Assume it is possible. Those two visions would offer real change across the whole industry, from copyright to production companies to film schools. There would be no point in camera rental companies, or in sophisticated editing software. The need for many people's expertise would go down the drain.

## Proposals

From here we can wander toward a general approach: media as something else.

If we research enough we might find that watching certain content improves health, and media could become a medicine for various problems. Instead of cough syrup we would be asked to watch a particular film for a few hours. Already there are people who see teenagers' high skill at computer games as a good reason to recruit them to the army for certain roles; games could be a platform for finding employees with relevant skills. The visions here are media as medicine and media as a hiring platform. It is relatively simple to formulate a role media has not been assigned until now, because its products are no longer considered purely leisure businesses.

The people working in the countless fields of media — art, technology, distribution, rights — need to ask what media can achieve that has not been achieved yet, and what properties it has and could have. At least around me, I do not hear enough voices proposing to walk the Completely New axis, alongside the Other and the Further.
