Film studies mostly discuss content. Representations of gender, race and sexuality have been at the centre of the discourse for years. When they are not, we tend to discuss outdated business models, distribution or technologies. We are still fascinated by Bazin's idea that the camera captures everything in front of the lens as an act of magic, while knowing perfectly well that visual effects deceive our perception — and they were doing it years before generative AI started doing the same.

It is time to move on and accept that the way we consume media has changed rapidly and radically. Remote controls, smart televisions, streaming, user interfaces, hidden algorithms. These belong in undergraduate film studies. I was not lucky enough to learn any of it in a course, so I explored a little on my own and wrote several seminars from that angle.

## 1. Quantitative analysis: the theatrical window

In a graduate seminar paper I argued that we need to talk about film preservation. One reason is that it has become almost impossible to keep up with the theatrical window — the period during which a film plays in cinemas, from its national premiere until it leaves for the next stage of its life, usually video on demand and community screenings.

The window used to be longer. Some sources put it at over six months in the 1990s. Successful films naturally get longer windows, since there are more tickets to sell.

## 2. User interface analysis: the Netflix home page

We think we have personal taste in art. We do not just watch a film at random; I like Almodóvar, not Cassavetes. That may be true, but let us not belittle the importance of how the Netflix screen is composed.

Above the top carousel sits the poster for one film. That poster is about thirty-two times larger than the posters beneath it — six and a half times wider, five times taller — and placed above them as a headline. Look again and the same film also appears inside the carousel. Netflix has given a great deal of its home page to one title.

That film was bought as something likely to keep subscribers satisfied. Their Adjusted Viewer Share algorithm is designed to make sure subscribers who are considering cancelling stay. They feed us what they want us to watch, to justify the acquisition. That way they can hold only a small fraction of the whole history of cinema, because none of us enjoys reading "we don't have that, but you might like…".

This is the tip of the iceberg. The lesson is to ask new, technological questions. How do they know me? Which categories am I offered? Why does content evaporate at that particular moment? If the service deletes this film, where else can I legally see it? The streaming services are working around the Paramount Decree, which forbids production companies from owning cinemas. If you do not believe me, try to find Bandersnatch anywhere outside a pirate site.

## 3. Cognitive experiments: MVP

How long does it take to identify which film we are seeing? Imagine switching on the television and trying to name the show in front of you. You look for clues — familiar faces, locations, known objects, the blue meth in Breaking Bad. Perhaps stylistic elements, an unusual camera angle, a particular colour grade. Whatever the show, it takes a moment, and you use your memory of past viewings.

We use the brain to compare geometry — types of face, room composition, object size — and to process colour. We build expectations, detect lies and construct plots. Over the last thirty years there has been an increase in content that requires rewatching, and content that asks us to fill gaps as we watch. Memento, Inception.

Old-school film theory cannot help here. I had the pleasure of studying a cognitive film theory seminar taught by Dr. Gal Raz, an Israeli psychology and film scholar who works on the connection between media and empathy. A year before that course I listed every film I had ever seen and started taking one frame from each. Soon I had a few hundred frames, which I assembled on a timeline giving each one a twenty-fourth of a second. Eventually it was a video of eight hundred frames, each the most representative frame of its film, in my opinion.

When the seminar paper came due I knew immediately what it would be about: frame recognition. After a conversation with Dr. Raz I had twenty-five people watch it separately and asked them to name the films they had seen. Then they were given a list of the films in the piece and asked to tick whether they had ever seen each one, and tick again if they thought they had seen a frame of it.

The result reinforced Miller's magic number seven: participants named roughly seven films, plus or minus two. It also showed that animated characters drawn with a minimal, saturated palette and belonging to a broad franchise are the most likely to be recognised. SpongeBob and Patrick, Shrek and Winnie the Pooh scored highest. Famous actors — Johnny Depp, Michael Jordan — also scored well. With the frames from Mike Myers' The Cat in the Hat and Mena Suvari's American Beauty it is hard to say what made them so recognisable: the high red saturation, the film's distribution, or the actors.

As far as I know, no student at the Tisch Film School has run an experiment this size or larger. We were never taught to examine films this way. I am aware that some groups abroad do.

The experiment was submitted to the Faculty of Arts for approval and was not declined, because it was not considered an experiment requiring approval. The narrow pool of participants — mostly art students in their twenties — means these results are guidelines and assumptions for further work. An attempt to get funding for larger experiments was made and refused.

## 4. Designing solutions: HydroCinema

We live in unusual times, when a cable in a hole in the wall makes electricity do its magic. For most of human history entertainment was expensive, and private entertainment more so. Anyone who wanted the best actors performing Shakespeare in their own yard, for family and friends alone, had to be wealthy or royal. Today I can open an app and watch them in the most private place there is, for nothing. Nobody guarantees it will stay that way. Perhaps in the future spending electricity on entertainment will be something to be embarrassed about.

When the film projector was first shown to the world it was hand-cranked, which means a film had no fixed frame rate and therefore no fixed running time. Edison fixed the frame rate with his projector. From that point on every projected frame used electricity. Even if the grid runs on solar, hydro, thermal or nuclear power, it is all converted into electricity, and every conversion loses energy.

I set myself the challenge of designing the first film projector that runs purely on the mechanical power of renewable energy. Two admissions first: I am not an engineer of any kind, nor a good mechanic, nor a draughtsman — just a film enthusiast with mad ideas. And I chose hydro after a short course on renewable energy.

Hydro is the only renewable source stable enough across any given two hours. Solar cannot produce at night, which is a favourite time for watching films. Wind is unpredictable. Nuclear is not safe next to an audience. Hydro can mean something as simple as a container of water draining into another over two hours, with a water wheel turning in between. That turn can become the turn of the film reels, supply and take-up.

It has problems I cannot solve right now: how to light the bulb; how to get the water back into the upper container afterwards; how to shoot films without electricity; how to distribute television and interactive media this way. If this were a real project, someone would have supplied the sharp minds needed. For now a simple diagram branded HydroCinema is all there is. But look at what else there is — attention to a new problem, and a new argument about how dependent our society is on media, which is dependent on electricity. How often do you find a film scholar interested in electricity?
