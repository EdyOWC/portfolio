Recently I watched Now You See Me: Now You Don't. I had very few expectations and it still disappointed me. The plot was generic and felt like an unnecessary reunion nobody really wanted to attend. On that particular film, the less I write the better.

But something did catch my attention: where each piece of magic in the film actually comes from. Spoilers ahead.

Early on we meet a group of young, undiversified magicians performing an event. They are pretending to be the Four Horsemen from the previous films, fooling the audience on stage. In one trick the Horsemen appear to transform into a volunteer's body. At the end their victim reveals that the figures on stage were only projections.

So here is the magic without the plot: several bodies become one body. And there is a gap in how the trick was made. For the diegetic audience, the people at the performance, it was done with projectors and no Horsemen involved at all. For the audience in the cinema it was done with a great deal of VFX and editing. We are not shocked that these characters were rendered so smoothly; we are confused by the reveal, that a large audience could not tell the difference. Secondhand magic.

## Three ways films make magic

In most films about magic it is all VFX and editing. Harry Potter's spells, the moving portraits, The Sorcerer's Apprentice. Learning to make a thunderbolt is one of the first things a visual effects editor does, in the first few weeks. It is handy for all sorts of other effects. Sometimes it even ships as a prefab, to speed things up.

A second way to excite us is to pose a riddle and solve it later, usually by hiding information and then supplying it during the solution. Guy Ritchie's Sherlock Holmes from 2009 does this repeatedly — Sherlock miraculously spots the ink on Mary's cheek, knows the bumps and smells of the streets of London, knows how to mix potions. In most cases we do not even get the clues he has, so if you paused the film at that point you could not solve it at home. There is a built-in gap between the information he holds and the information we lack. The same happens in Knives Out and The Prestige. Oddly, when you read Conan Doyle's stories you do have everything you need, and it is still hard to solve the case yourself.

Some films let us solve the riddle at home, and ask us to keep looking. Here the gap sits between what the film gives us, driven by the narrative, and what another source — a website, say — can give. That is where transmedia storytelling gets its opening: start with a film, continue on a website, finish with an event. The Blair Witch Project made its audience believe the actors really were missing.

## Twelve frames

After seeing the new one I remembered the opening scene of the trilogy. The first trick made me appreciate the whole film, and as my generation says, it lived rent-free in my head for more than a decade.

In that scene Jay Atlas fans a deck of cards for a very short moment. He asks the woman in front of him to pick a card from the deck she just saw, and through her he asks us, the audience, to pick one. Then he shows the cards again. We have a card in mind: the seven of diamonds. He runs through some cards and asks whether hers is among them. It is not. He points at the building beside them, and there it is in bright lights — the seven of diamonds.

Break it down. The scene shows us several cards and the film somehow knows which one we will pick. At the end the seven of diamonds is on the building, surprising all of us, which means we all chose the same card. The film has only just begun, so it cannot have implanted the card in our memory the way it does to a character later in the plot. There are no sensors in the cinema tracking our collective gaze and tailoring the ending to our behaviour.

A pause. In my first year at university a student made an interactive film using a Tobii eye-tracking camera, where the narrative was shaped by what the viewer looked at. It was pre-recorded, so there was a fixed number of endings and we never see them all. We talked after a test and he told me the film could predict whether someone was straight or gay, preferred wine or weed, and so on. An ambitious claim, but I knew what he meant: by looking at an object we desire it, and a computer can calculate which segment to show next. A simple A/B algorithm, easy to implement in a game engine. As far as I know this has not yet reached mass cinema.

So there must be a reason the magic works. I got hold of the scene and went through the two shots of the trick frame by frame, counting which cards appear on screen.

Watching it the first time you do not notice, but the seven of diamonds is present in twenty-nine per cent of the frames, more than any other card. In total that is only twelve frames out of forty-one. The whole film runs to around a hundred and seventy thousand frames, so twelve is nothing. Those twelve frames make the difference, and they are enough to plant that card — that thought, some would say — in our minds. We look at the logos of real companies in films for considerably longer than twelve frames. That may be enough to make us think a film is artistic while it advertises.

The magic here rests on cognition. We are exposed to a range of images that steer the mind toward what someone wants it to think. Showing the seven of diamonds seventy per cent of the time would make us wonder whether the whole deck is that card. Showing it three per cent of the time, barely more than the 1.9 per cent a single card earns in a normal deck, would not do the trick. Somebody worked this out and tested it many times before it went into the script. Cognitive film theory is the key to making this kind of film.

Now You See Me: Now You Don't has a similar pick-a-card trick. Five cards in front of us, choose one, and a moment later it appears somewhere else. For me they pulled it off. I was fascinated and asked the friend beside me whether it had worked for him too. He nodded. I want to know why they were right, and why only for me — a simple guess, the position in the frame, something subconscious. There is a reason, and I will go looking for it when the film is available elsewhere.

The Horsemen say the closer you look, the less you see. I say the slower you observe, the more you discover.
